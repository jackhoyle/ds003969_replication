\title{Replicating Braboszcz et al.\ (2017) on OpenNeuro \texttt{ds003969}:\\
pipeline decisions, sensitivity, and a look at criticality}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
I rebuilt the EEG pipeline of Braboszcz et al.\ (2017, \emph{PLoS ONE} 12:e0170647) in MNE-Python
and applied it to the public release of their data. The alpha result reproduces:
Vipassana practitioners have more 7--11\,Hz power than the other meditation groups during meditation,
and this holds under every pipeline variant tried. The headline gamma (60--110\,Hz) excess in meditators
over controls is less robust. Its sign always agrees with the paper, but it is clearly significant only when
no ICA components are removed. Automated muscle-component removal roughly halves it.
I then computed long-range temporal correlations (DFA) and functional E/I (fEI) with the parameters of
the recent preprint ``Near-critical brain dynamics track effortlessness during meditation''.
As in that preprint, there are no meditator--control differences. The one apparent within-meditator
effect (beta DFA higher in the second meditation block than in the first) also appears, and is larger,
in controls. It is therefore best read as a time-on-task effect, not as an effect of meditative depth.
\end{abstract}

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Data and target results}

The dataset (\texttt{ds003969}) contains 64-channel Biosemi recordings of 98 participants: 31 controls (CTR),
24 Himalayan Yoga (HTR, ``HYT'' in the paper), 20 Isha Shoonya (SNY, ``ISY''), 19 Vipassana (VIP) and
4 Transcendental Meditation (TM). Each participant did a 20-minute instructed mind-wandering (IMW) session and
a 20-minute meditation session (MED). These are released as four $\sim$10-minute files: \texttt{think1},
\texttt{think2}, \texttt{med1breath} (breath focus) and \texttt{med2} (the tradition's own practice;
controls keep doing breath focus). The paper analysed the last 10 minutes of each session, which here are
\texttt{think2} and \texttt{med2}.

The results targeted were:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gamma (60--110\,Hz).} Higher parieto-occipital power in all three meditator groups than in
        controls, both as a trait (MED and IMW combined) and during MED (Fig.~\ref{fig:paper}b).
  \item \textbf{Alpha (7--11\,Hz).} During MED, VIP higher than CTR, ISY and HYT.
  \item (Not attempted) the correlation between gamma and log practice hours (Fig.~4D).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where results are most likely to diverge.} In rough order of expected impact:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{ICA component selection.} The paper removed 1--5 components chosen by eye. The first automated run
        removed more than 10 of 20 components in many recordings (Fig.~\ref{fig:ica}a). Section~\ref{sec:sens}
        shows that this step controls the gamma result.
  \item \textbf{Sample composition.} The replication uses about 50\% more participants and does not age-match.
        HTR is on average 11 years older than CTR. High-frequency EEG power and EMG both change with age, so the
        gamma contrast for HTR is confounded with age in a way the paper's matched design avoided.
  \item \textbf{Bad channels.} The automated detector removes slightly more channels than the paper did
        (6.9 vs $\approx$5 on average). Channels are dropped, not interpolated, so the parieto-occipital average is
        taken over a cluster whose make-up varies from subject to subject.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} The cluster permutation approach was not re-implemented. The tests here are uncorrected
        tests on one predefined cluster, so their $p$-values are not directly comparable to the paper's.
  \item \textbf{Unreproducible manual steps.} The paper's \texttt{pop\_rejcont} threshold tweaks and its channel and
        component choices cannot be recovered from the text.
\end{enumerate}

Two smaller points came up along the way:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The paper's y-axis ($\sim$45\,dB at low frequencies) is offset from a correctly scaled PSD in
        $\mu$V$^2$/Hz by a constant of roughly 35--45\,dB. This is consistent with an unnormalised squared FFT
        magnitude. Because it is a constant offset in dB, between-group differences are unaffected.
  \item The 100\,Hz line-noise harmonic falls inside the gamma band and is not excluded. Visual inspection showed
        nothing abnormal at 100\,Hz.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Gamma.} Across every variant the meditator-minus-control difference is positive for all three
groups, so the direction of the paper's result reproduces. Its size and significance depend on ICA:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No ICA:} trait differences of +3.3\,dB (HTR, $p=.002$), +2.2\,dB (SNY, $p=.03$) and
        +1.9\,dB (VIP, $p=.12$). During MED, HTR and SNY are both significant at $p\le.02$. This is close to
        the paper's picture.
  \item \textbf{Automated ICA:} the differences shrink to 0.6--2.0\,dB and are at most marginal. VIP is hit
        hardest, falling from +2.2 to $\approx$0\,dB during MED.
\end{itemize}

The log's working hypothesis (8 Oct) was that EOG component removal drives the discrepancy. The data point
the other way. Raising the EOG threshold removes about 0.9 fewer EOG components per recording but changes
gamma little: HTR trait goes from 1.6 to 2.0\,dB and VIP from 0.8 to 0.6\,dB. Most of the gap between the
ICA runs and no ICA is explained by the $\sim$2.7 \emph{muscle} components per recording that remain.
The number of muscle components removed is similar across groups (2.0--2.6 per recording), so the
attenuation does not come from removing more components in meditators. It comes from \emph{what} those
components contain.

Two readings fit this:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The ``muscle'' detector correctly removes EMG that is more prevalent in meditators. The paper's gamma
        effect would then be partly artefactual.
  \item The detector also captures genuine broadband high-frequency activity, or microsaccadic spike potentials,
        which a spectral-slope muscle criterion cannot tell apart from EMG.
\end{enumerate}
The paper's own control analysis is relevant here: its eye-component cluster correlated with parieto-occipital
gamma ($r=0.33$), whereas temporal-muscle components did not. The automated pipeline cannot settle this
question. Careful manual labelling of a subset of recordings could; this was started on 8 Oct (19 recordings
in \texttt{processed\_manual}). Early impressions were that the muscle suggestions look sensible and the EOG
suggestions often need correcting.

\paragraph{Alpha.} VIP minus the other meditators during MED is +3.0 to +3.3\,dB ($p=.006$--$.014$) under
every pipeline, and is similar for IMW and for the trait average. This part of the paper's alpha result is
robust. VIP minus CTR is +1.6 to +2.1\,dB but not significant ($p=.07$--$.17$), whereas the paper reports
$p<.01$. The likeliest explanations are the unmatched and larger control group, and the cluster-level
statistics.

\section{Criticality: DFA and fEI}

\paragraph{Method.} The Hilbert amplitude envelope was computed in delta, theta, alpha (8--13\,Hz),
beta (13--30\,Hz), broadband (1--30\,Hz) and (no-ICA run only) gamma. DFA and fEI used \texttt{crosci} on
non-overlapping 180\,s epochs, with DFA fitted over 5--30\,s and fEI computed in 5\,s windows with 80\%
overlap. Values were averaged over epochs and good channels to give one value per subject, task and band.
Manual implementations of DFA and fEI were first written and checked against \texttt{crosci}; they differed
slightly because of windowing.

Contrasts were fitted as linear mixed models with a random intercept per subject. The project notebook used
WLS with HC3 errors and treated subject--task rows as independent; the conclusions are the same.
TM was excluded.

\paragraph{Data loss.} An epoch is dropped if \emph{any} \texttt{BAD} span overlaps it. Even a 2\,s
\texttt{pop\_rejcont} flag therefore discards 180\,s of data. Consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Only 232 of 392 recordings have at least one usable epoch (222 non-TM, from 83 subjects).
  \item \texttt{think2} retains only 43 of 98 recordings.
  \item Usable recordings average 1.7 epochs, about 5 of 10 minutes.
  \item Only 37 meditators have both meditation blocks.
\end{itemize}
This is the most important practical weakness of the criticality analysis. Possible fixes are 50\%-overlapping
windows (as in the preprint), computing DFA on concatenated clean data, or a less aggressive rejection rule.
An epoch-length sweep (40--300\,s, \texttt{exploration/261012\_*}) has been written, but the saved output covers
only 2 recordings and is not yet informative.

\paragraph{Results} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dfa}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MED vs IMW in meditators:} no difference in any band (alpha and beta estimates $<0.01$, $p>.5$).
  \item \textbf{Meditators vs controls:} no difference during IMW or MED in any band from theta to broadband
        ($|\Delta|<0.02$, $p>.5$). The CIs rule out differences larger than about 0.06--0.08 DFA units,
        roughly 10\% of the mean exponent of $\approx$0.7.
  \item \textbf{fEI:} nothing reaches $p<.05$ in any contrast or band except gamma.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{med2} vs \texttt{med1breath} in meditators:} beta DFA is higher in the second block,
        by +0.032 to +0.048 ($p=.007$--$.051$ depending on pipeline). Alpha shows the same direction but is not
        significant.
  \item \textbf{Gamma (no-ICA run only):} DFA and fEI are higher in meditators than controls during MED
        ($p=.013$, $p=.028$). Given the ICA sensitivity of gamma power above, this should be treated as
        artefact-prone until shown otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The \texttt{med2} effect is probably time-on-task.} \texttt{med2} always follows \texttt{med1breath},
and controls do breath focus in both blocks. If the rise reflected deepening practice, controls should not show
it. They do, and more strongly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Beta DFA rises by +0.075 to +0.077 in controls ($p=.005$--$.009$, $n=28$).
  \item Alpha DFA rises by +0.085 to +0.094 in controls ($p=.02$--$.05$).
  \item The block $\times$ group interaction is not significant for beta, and for alpha it is \emph{negative}
        (meditators rise less).
\end{itemize}
DFA also rises from \texttt{think1} to \texttt{think2}: for meditators' alpha by about +0.05 ($p=.02$--$.03$),
and in a smaller set of controls by a similar amount. The simplest account is that LRTCs in the alpha and beta
envelopes increase over the course of any 20-minute eyes-closed block. Drowsiness is the obvious candidate
mechanism.

Multiplicity reinforces this reading. About 40 contrasts were examined per pipeline (5 bands $\times$ 2 metrics
$\times$ 4 contrasts) across 4 pipelines. A single $p\approx.007$ would not survive a Bonferroni or BH correction.

\section{Comparison with ``Near-critical brain dynamics track effortlessness during meditation''}

Lewis-Healey, Kringelbach, Canales-Johnson and Laukkonen (bioRxiv, Aug 2026) recorded 128-channel EEG from
33 TM practitioners and 33 matched controls. Sessions consisted of a 10-minute rest, then 30 minutes of TM or
silent counting, then a further 10-minute rest. Effort was rated retrospectively over time with Temporal
Experience Tracing. Their criticality settings are essentially those used here: \texttt{crosci}, the same five
bands, 3-minute windows, DFA up to 30\,s, fEI with 5\,s windows at 80\% overlap, and fEI only where DFA $>0.6$.
They differ in a few respects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 50\% window overlap.
  \item Band-specific minimum DFA windows (alpha 3.98\,s, beta 2.24\,s).
  \item Average reference, 0.5--45\,Hz FIR filtering, FastICA with manual component rejection.
  \item Rejection on 5\,s epochs.
\end{itemize}
The dataset is different. The TM group in \texttt{ds003969} has only 4 participants and was not analysed here.

\paragraph{Agreement.} The preprint finds \emph{no robust between-group differences} in LRTCs or fEI, during
either rest or practice. The replication agrees across all bands and pipelines, with three meditation
traditions instead of one. The preprint also finds no fEI condition effects after FDR correction, which matches
the null fEI results here.

\paragraph{Apparent disagreement.} Within meditators, the preprint finds TM increases alpha and beta DFA relative
to rest both \emph{before and after}. Here, MED does not differ from IMW. The comparisons are not equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
  \item IMW is an active, effortful recall task rather than rest, so it is a weaker contrast for a hypothesis
        about ``letting go''.
  \item The preprint's pre/post bracketing controls for slow drift. The MED-vs-IMW comparison here does not;
        session order is counterbalanced only at the group level, and much data has been lost.
\end{itemize}
The bracketing design matters. The \texttt{med2}-vs-\texttt{med1breath} analysis above shows that drift within
a block is large enough (about +0.08 DFA units over 10 minutes in controls) to produce a spurious ``deepening''
effect when blocks are not counterbalanced.

\paragraph{What this data can and cannot say about effortlessness.} The preprint's main finding is
within-person: beta LRTCs rise as reported effort falls, more so in meditators. The strongest effect is beta
(standardised $\beta=-0.28$), and meditators show higher beta LRTCs only in their lowest-effort windows.
\texttt{ds003969} has no time-resolved phenomenology, so this cannot be tested directly. The nearest proxy, the
move from effortful breath focus to the tradition's own practice, does show a beta-DFA increase. It is not
specific to meditators, however, so it cannot be attributed to effortlessness. Two points follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Time-on-task drift is a plausible confound for any effort--LRTC association, because reported effort
        also tends to fall over a session. The preprint addresses this with time-control models, and its effects
        survive (alpha $\beta=-0.47$, beta $\beta=-0.50$).
  \item The size of the drift seen here ($\sim$0.08 in 10 minutes) is a useful benchmark for how strong that
        control must be.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary and next steps}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Alpha (VIP $>$ other traditions): replicates}, and is insensitive to every pipeline decision tried.
  \item \textbf{Gamma (meditators $>$ controls): direction replicates; strength does not.} It is significant
        without ICA, but automated muscle-component removal halves it. The cause is the content of the removed
        components, not how many are removed.
  \item \textbf{Criticality: null between groups}, consistent with the near-criticality preprint. The one
        within-meditator effect is most parsimoniously explained by time-on-task.
\end{itemize}

Next steps, in priority order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Re-run the spectral comparison on an age- and gender-matched subsample of 16 per group.
  \item Manually label ICA components for a stratified subset of recordings and compare the gamma result with
        the automated choices, looking specifically for saccadic spike-potential components.
  \item Reduce data loss in DFA/fEI with overlapping windows or concatenated clean segments, and finish the
        epoch-length sweep.
  \item Replace the uncorrected tests with cluster permutation tests, or at least FDR correction across bands
        and contrasts.
\end{enumerate}
