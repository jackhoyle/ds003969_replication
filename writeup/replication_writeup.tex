\title{Replicating Braboszcz et al.\ (2017) on OpenNeuro \texttt{ds003969}:\\
pipeline decisions, sensitivity, and a look at criticality}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
I rebuilt the EEG pipeline of Braboszcz et al.\ (2017, \emph{PLoS ONE} 12:e0170647) in MNE-Python
and applied it to the public release of their data, restricted to the paper's own age- and gender-matched
sample, which I recovered from the authors' Zenodo deposit. With no ICA component removal, the replication
reproduces the paper closely: per-subject parieto-occipital gamma correlates with the paper's published values
at $r=0.97$, and the group differences agree to within 0.6\,dB. Both headline results replicate. The gamma
(60--110\,Hz) excess in meditators over controls is about 4\,dB during meditation. Vipassana practitioners have
more 7--11\,Hz alpha than every other group. Automated ICA component removal shrinks the gamma effect,
from about 4 to 2.5\,dB, though the pooled meditator--control difference stays significant. The paper's own values
track the no-ICA data, so its manual ICA left parieto-occipital gamma largely untouched. Whether the extra power
removed by automated ICA is artefact or signal remains open.
I then computed long-range temporal correlations (DFA) and functional E/I (fEI) with the parameters of
the recent preprint ``Near-critical brain dynamics track effortlessness during meditation''.
As in that preprint, there are no meditator--control differences. The one apparent within-meditator
effect (beta DFA higher in the second meditation block than in the first) appears equally in controls.
It is therefore best read as a time-on-task effect, not as an effect of meditative depth.
\end{abstract}

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Data and target results}

The dataset (\texttt{ds003969}) contains 64-channel Biosemi recordings of 98 participants: 31 controls (CTR),
24 Himalayan Yoga (HTR, ``HYT'' in the paper), 20 Isha Shoonya (SNY, ``ISY''), 19 Vipassana (VIP) and
4 Transcendental Meditation (TM). Each participant did a 20-minute instructed mind-wandering (IMW) session and
a 20-minute meditation session (MED). These are released as four $\sim$10-minute files: \texttt{think1},
\texttt{think2}, \texttt{med1breath} (breath focus) and \texttt{med2} (the tradition's own practice;
controls keep doing breath focus). The paper analysed the last 10 minutes of each session, which here are
\texttt{think2} and \texttt{med2}.

The recording start times in the BDF headers show how the blocks were scheduled. Both blocks of a session were
recorded back to back (median gap under a minute), and the two sessions were separated by a median of 66\,minutes
(range 47--89), all on one day. Half of the participants did the MED session first and half the IMW session first,
as recorded in the \texttt{first\_session} column of \texttt{participants.tsv}; this agrees with the timestamps for
all 98. Within a session the order is fixed, so \texttt{med2} always directly follows \texttt{med1breath}.
(The dataset README describes the blocks as alternating, which the timestamps contradict.)

The paper analysed 64 participants, 16 per group, matched on age and gender from a larger pool. The dataset does
not record which participants these were. The authors' earlier Zenodo deposit (doi 10.5281/zenodo.57911) lists
them by lab ID, along with their per-subject median gamma and alpha values. I mapped these lab IDs to
\texttt{ds003969} subjects using the recording dates in the BDF headers, cross-checked against age.
63 of the 64 are in \texttt{ds003969}; HYT26's recording was never converted. All results in this report use
this matched sample (16 CTR, 15 HYT, 16 ISY, 16 VIP). The pipeline restricts to it with
\texttt{run\_pipeline.py --paper-subjects}.

The results targeted were:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gamma (60--110\,Hz).} Higher parieto-occipital power in all three meditator groups than in
        controls, both as a trait (MED and IMW combined) and during MED (the paper's Fig.~3, reproduced as
        Fig.~\ref{fig:paper}).
  \item \textbf{Alpha (7--11\,Hz).} During MED, VIP higher than CTR, ISY and HYT.
  \item (Not re-analysed; see the note in Section~\ref{sec:matched}) the correlation between gamma and log practice
        hours (Fig.~4D).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where results are most likely to diverge.} In rough order of expected impact:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{ICA component selection.} The paper removed 1--5 components chosen by eye. The automated selection
        controls the size of the gamma result (Section~\ref{sec:matched}).
  \item \textbf{Bad channels.} The automated detector removes slightly more channels than the paper did
        (6.8 vs $\approx$5 on average). Channels are dropped, not interpolated, so the parieto-occipital average is
        taken over a cluster whose make-up varies from subject to subject.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} The cluster permutation approach was not re-implemented. The tests here are uncorrected
        tests on one predefined cluster, so their $p$-values are not directly comparable to the paper's.
  \item \textbf{Unreproducible manual steps.} The paper's \texttt{pop\_rejcont} threshold tweaks and its channel and
        component choices cannot be recovered from the text.
\end{enumerate}

Two smaller points came up along the way:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The paper's y-axis ($\sim$45\,dB at low frequencies) is offset from a correctly scaled PSD in
        $\mu$V$^2$/Hz by a constant of roughly 35--45\,dB. This is consistent with an unnormalised squared FFT
        magnitude. Because it is a constant offset in dB, between-group differences are unaffected.
  \item The 100\,Hz line-noise harmonic falls inside the gamma band and is not excluded. Visual inspection showed
        nothing abnormal at 100\,Hz.
\end{itemize}

\section{How the final pipeline was reached}
\label{sec:process}

Two decisions changed during the project. Results from the superseded versions are not reported here.

\paragraph{Sample.} The project was first run on all 94 non-TM participants, because the dataset does not say
which participants the paper analysed. That sample is not the paper's: it has about 50\% more participants, is
not age-matched, and its additional controls are younger than the paper's. Its group comparisons therefore do not
test the paper's claims, and they gave a misleading picture of how well the paper replicates. Recovering the
paper's subject list from its Zenodo deposit (Section~1) fixed this, and the spectral and criticality pipelines
were rerun on that list. Because each recording is processed independently, the matched-sample spectra are
identical (0.000\,dB difference) to the earlier runs' values for the same subjects; only the participants
compared have changed.

\paragraph{ICA.} The paper's component choices were made by eye and cannot be recovered, so several automated
rules were tried while developing the pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An uncapped rule (muscle threshold 0.5, EOG $z{>}3$) often removed more than 10 of 20 components per
        recording, far more than the paper's 1--5.
  \item Raising the thresholds (muscle 1.0, EOG $z{>}5$) removed essentially nothing.
  \item An intermediate uncapped rule (muscle 0.75, EOG $z{>}3$) removed a median of about 2 but up to 17.
  \item The rule adopted caps the count to mirror the paper: at least one muscle component, at most four EOG and at
        most five in total. Variants with a higher EOG threshold ($z{>}4$) and with the EOG channels filtered
        before scoring were also tried.
\end{itemize}
Because the gamma result proved sensitive to ICA, the final analysis runs the capped rule
(\texttt{\_matchsample\_v0}) and a no-ICA pipeline (\texttt{\_matchsample\_noica\_v0}) side by side. A manual
labelling of components was started on 19 recordings (\texttt{processed\_manual}), and the pipeline now has a
manual mode for this.

\paragraph{Without ICA, the paper is reproduced.} Table~\ref{tab:matched} and Fig.~\ref{fig:matched} show
the no-ICA run agrees with the paper's own numbers to within 0.6\,dB for every gamma contrast.
All three meditator groups exceed controls during MED, and the four-group ANOVA is significant.
The match also holds subject by subject (Fig.~\ref{fig:persubject}a):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gamma: $r=0.97$ in MED and 0.93 in MW.
  \item Alpha: $r=0.97$.
  \item The residual scatter around a constant offset is 1.1--1.7\,dB per subject.
\end{itemize}
This is strong evidence that the reimplemented pipeline is faithful to the original, from referencing through
segment rejection to spectral estimation. It is a reproduction on the same data, not an independent replication.

\paragraph{Automated ICA attenuates gamma.} With the capped ICA, the gamma contrasts shrink:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The pooled meditator--control difference falls from about 4 to 2.5\,dB and remains significant
        ($p=.034$ in MED, $p=.023$ trait).
  \item The four-group ANOVA is no longer significant.
  \item Only ISY survives individually.
\end{itemize}
The attenuation is concentrated in a few recordings. In MED the median subject loses only 0.2\,dB, but
8 of 63 subjects lose more than 3\,dB (3.9--16.9\,dB), and 7 of those 8 are meditators (Fig.~\ref{fig:persubject}b).
These recordings did not have unusually many components removed: half of them lost only one or two, a mix of
muscle and EOG components. Across all recordings the number of muscle components removed is similar between groups
(1.9--2.6 per recording). So the attenuation comes from \emph{what} a few components contain in a few meditators,
not from removing more components in meditators.

The paper's manual ICA evidently did not remove this power, because its values track the no-ICA data. Two
readings fit:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The automated detectors correctly remove EMG or eye-related activity that is present in these meditators.
        The paper's gamma effect would then be partly artefactual.
  \item The detectors also capture genuine broadband high-frequency activity, or microsaccadic spike potentials,
        which a spectral-slope muscle criterion cannot tell apart from EMG.
\end{enumerate}
The paper's own control analysis is relevant here: its eye-component cluster correlated with parieto-occipital
gamma ($r=0.33$), whereas temporal-muscle components did not. Labelling the components removed in these 8 recordings
by hand is the direct test.

\paragraph{Alpha replicates.} VIP exceeds CTR by 3.1--3.7\,dB ($p=.012$--$.037$), matching the
paper's +3.5\,dB. VIP also exceeds the other two traditions by 3.6--3.8\,dB ($p\le.007$), under both pipelines.

\paragraph{Age.} Within the matched sample (no ICA, trait gamma), gamma tends to \emph{fall} with age in controls
(Spearman $\rho=-0.45$, $p=.08$, $n=16$) but \emph{rise} with age in meditators ($\rho=+0.26$, $p=.08$, $n=47$).
Neither trend is significant on its own, but together they mean the size of the group difference depends on the
age range sampled. Age-matching, as the paper did, is therefore essential to the comparison.

\paragraph{A note on the experience correlation (Fig.~4D).} Practice hours rise with age, so the age trend in
meditators bears on the paper's correlation between gamma and log practice hours. In the paper's own per-subject
data for the 48 meditators:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gamma correlates with age ($r=0.27$, $p=.07$) more than with log hours ($r=0.16$, $p=.27$).
  \item Log hours and age correlate with each other ($r=0.38$).
  \item With age in the model, log hours explains nothing ($p=.63$).
\end{itemize}
The published correlation used a skipped correlation over a different subset of conditions, so this is not a
re-analysis of it. It does suggest the experience effect cannot be separated from age in this sample.

\section{Criticality: DFA and fEI}

\paragraph{Method.} The Hilbert amplitude envelope was computed in theta, alpha (8--13\,Hz),
beta (13--30\,Hz), broadband (1--30\,Hz) and gamma (60--110\,Hz). DFA and fEI used \texttt{crosci} on
non-overlapping 180\,s epochs, with DFA fitted over 5--30\,s and fEI computed in 5\,s windows with 80\%
overlap. Values were averaged over epochs and good channels to give one value per subject, task and band.
Manual implementations of DFA and fEI were first written and checked against \texttt{crosci}; they differed
slightly because of windowing.

Contrasts were fitted as linear mixed models with a random intercept per subject, for both pipelines
(\texttt{scripts/matched\_criticality.py}; results in \texttt{results/matched/matched\_criticality.csv}).

\paragraph{Data loss.} An epoch is dropped if \emph{any} \texttt{BAD} span overlaps it. Even a 2\,s
\texttt{pop\_rejcont} flag therefore discards 180\,s of data. Consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Only 149 of 252 recordings (59\%) have at least one usable epoch, from 55 of the 63 subjects.
  \item \texttt{think2} retains only 28 of 63 recordings.
  \item Usable recordings average 1.7 epochs, about 5 of 10 minutes.
  \item Only 26 meditators and 6 controls have both meditation blocks. The mixed models also use subjects with one
        block, but the within-subject contrasts rest mainly on these.
\end{itemize}
This is the most important practical weakness of the criticality analysis. An epoch-length sweep on the same
recordings (\texttt{exploration/261012\_*}) shows how much shorter epochs would recover: 95\% of recordings keep at
least one epoch at 60\,s and 88\% at 90\,s, against 59\% at 180\,s. Other possible fixes are 50\%-overlapping windows
(as in the preprint), computing DFA on concatenated clean data, or placing epochs between bad spans instead of on a
fixed grid.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/matched_dfa.pdf}
\caption{DFA contrasts (mixed-model estimates, 95\% CIs) under both pipelines. ``Meditators'' means HYT, ISY
and VIP pooled. MED $-$ IMW pools both blocks of each condition.}
\label{fig:dfa}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Results} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dfa}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MED vs IMW in meditators:} no DFA difference in any band ($|\Delta|<0.02$, $p>.48$).
  \item \textbf{Meditators vs controls:} no DFA difference during IMW or MED in any band from theta to broadband
        ($p>.26$). During MED the CIs rule out differences larger than about 0.07 DFA units, roughly 10\% of the mean
        exponent of $\approx$0.7. During IMW, where fewer recordings survive, they only rule out differences larger
        than about 0.15.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{med2} vs \texttt{med1breath} in meditators:} beta DFA is higher in the second block,
        by +0.052 to +0.059 ($p=.013$--$.034$ depending on pipeline). Broadband shows the same direction
        (+0.023 to +0.035, $p=.04$--$.20$) and alpha is not significant.
  \item \textbf{fEI:} no meditator--control differences. Within meditators, alpha fEI is slightly lower during MED
        than IMW ($-0.04$, $p=.036$--$.049$), and beta fEI likewise under the capped ICA only ($p=.045$). In controls,
        fEI falls from \texttt{med1breath} to \texttt{med2} in alpha and beta ($p=.013$--$.053$).
  \item \textbf{Gamma:} meditators' DFA and fEI tend to be higher than controls' during MED (+0.07 to +0.08,
        $p=.09$--$.11$). Given the ICA sensitivity of gamma power above, any such difference should be treated as
        artefact-prone until shown otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The \texttt{med2} effect is probably time-on-task.} \texttt{med2} always directly follows
\texttt{med1breath}, and controls do breath focus in both blocks. If the rise reflected deepening practice, controls
should not show it. They do, by the same amount:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Beta DFA rises by +0.049 to +0.053 in controls ($p=.025$--$.057$; 14 controls, 6 with both blocks).
  \item The block $\times$ meditator interaction is essentially zero for beta ($+0.009$ and $-0.004$, $p>.8$),
        and negative but not significant for alpha.
\end{itemize}
The simplest account is that LRTCs in the beta envelope increase over the course of a 20-minute eyes-closed
session. Drowsiness is the obvious candidate mechanism.

Multiplicity reinforces this reading. 120 contrasts were tested (6 tests $\times$ 5 bands $\times$ 2 metrics
$\times$ 2 pipelines), and 10 reach $p<.05$, against about 6 expected by chance. They are scattered across bands and
tests, and none would survive a Bonferroni or BH correction.

\section{Comparison with ``Near-critical brain dynamics track effortlessness during meditation''}

Lewis-Healey, Kringelbach, Canales-Johnson and Laukkonen (bioRxiv, Aug 2026) recorded 128-channel EEG from
33 TM practitioners and 33 matched controls. Sessions consisted of a 10-minute rest, then 30 minutes of TM or
silent counting, then a further 10-minute rest. Effort was rated retrospectively over time with Temporal
Experience Tracing. Their criticality settings are essentially those used here: \texttt{crosci}, the same five
bands, 3-minute windows, DFA up to 30\,s, fEI with 5\,s windows at 80\% overlap, and fEI only where DFA $>0.6$.
They differ in a few respects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 50\% window overlap.
  \item Band-specific minimum DFA windows (alpha 3.98\,s, beta 2.24\,s).
  \item Average reference, 0.5--45\,Hz FIR filtering, FastICA with manual component rejection.
  \item Rejection on 5\,s epochs.
\end{itemize}
The dataset is different. The TM group in \texttt{ds003969} has only 4 participants and is not in the paper's
matched sample.

\paragraph{Agreement.} The preprint finds \emph{no robust between-group differences} in LRTCs or fEI, during
either rest or practice. The replication agrees across all bands and both pipelines, with three meditation
traditions instead of one. The preprint also finds no fEI condition effects after FDR correction; the few
uncorrected fEI effects here would not survive such a correction either.

\paragraph{Apparent disagreement.} Within meditators, the preprint finds TM increases alpha and beta DFA relative
to rest both \emph{before and after}. Here, MED does not differ from IMW. The comparisons are not equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
  \item IMW is an active, effortful recall task rather than rest, so it is a weaker contrast for a hypothesis
        about ``letting go''.
  \item The preprint's pre/post bracketing controls for slow drift. The MED-vs-IMW comparison here does not: the
        sessions are counterbalanced across subjects but separated by about an hour, and much data has been lost.
\end{itemize}
The bracketing design matters. The \texttt{med2}-vs-\texttt{med1breath} analysis above shows that drift within
a session is large enough (about +0.05 DFA units in beta over 10 minutes, in controls as well as meditators) to
produce a spurious ``deepening'' effect when blocks are not counterbalanced.

\paragraph{What this data can and cannot say about effortlessness.} The preprint's main finding is
within-person: beta LRTCs rise as reported effort falls, more so in meditators. The strongest effect is beta
(standardised $\beta=-0.28$), and meditators show higher beta LRTCs only in their lowest-effort windows.
\texttt{ds003969} has no time-resolved phenomenology, so this cannot be tested directly. The nearest proxy, the
move from effortful breath focus to the tradition's own practice, does show a beta-DFA increase. It is not
specific to meditators, however, so it cannot be attributed to effortlessness. Two points follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Time-on-task drift is a plausible confound for any effort--LRTC association, because reported effort
        also tends to fall over a session. The preprint addresses this with time-control models, and its effects
        survive (alpha $\beta=-0.47$, beta $\beta=-0.50$).
  \item The size of the drift seen here ($\sim$0.05 in 10 minutes) is a useful benchmark for how strong that
        control must be.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary and next steps}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The pipeline reproduces the paper.} On the paper's matched sample and without ICA, per-subject
        gamma matches the published values at $r=0.97$.
  \item \textbf{Gamma (meditators $>$ controls): replicates}, by about 4\,dB during MED.
        Automated ICA shrinks it to about 2.5\,dB, still significant when pooled. The shrinkage is concentrated in
        8 recordings, mostly meditators. The paper's manual ICA left parieto-occipital gamma essentially untouched.
  \item \textbf{Alpha (VIP $>$ all other groups): replicates} under both pipelines.
  \item \textbf{Age matters.} Controls' gamma tends to fall with age while meditators' rises, so effect size
        depends on the age range. The paper's experience correlation looks confounded with age.
  \item \textbf{Criticality: null between groups}, consistent with the near-criticality preprint. The one
        within-meditator effect is most parsimoniously explained by time-on-task.
\end{itemize}

Next steps, in priority order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Hand-label the ICA components behind the 8 recordings that lose more than 3\,dB of gamma under automated
        ICA, looking specifically for EMG and saccadic spike-potential components. This decides whether the
        removed power is artefact or signal.
  \item Reduce data loss in DFA/fEI with shorter or overlapping windows, or epochs placed between bad spans, and
        rerun the criticality contrasts.
  \item Replace the uncorrected tests with cluster permutation tests, or at least FDR correction across bands
        and contrasts.
\end{enumerate}
